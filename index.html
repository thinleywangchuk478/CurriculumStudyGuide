རྩ་གཞུང་གི་སྐོར། Curriculum – An Overview

Core Definition: རྩ་གཞུང་ (Curriculum) is the fundamental guiding document of education — encompassing aims, content, teaching methods, and learning outcomes. It is the master plan for achieving national educational goals.

1.1 རྩ་གཞུང་གི་གོ་དོན། Definition

རྩ་གཞུང་ཟེར་མི་འདི་ ཤེས་ཡོན་གྱི་ལམ་སྟོན་རྩ་བའི་ཡིག་ཆ། དམིགས་ཡུལ། ནང་དོན། སློབ་སྟོན་ཐབས་ཤེས། སློབ་འབྲས་ཚུ་ཚང་སྟེ་འོངམ།

Curriculum is the guiding document of education — containing learning objectives, content, teaching methods, and learning outcomes. It also refers to the foundational framework that outlines educational purpose and activities aligned with national goals.

Types of Curriculum

1.2 Prescribed Curriculum

Official curriculum set by authorities (e.g. MoE Bhutan). Planned content, objectives, and standards teachers must follow.

Top-downOfficial

1.3 Taught Curriculum

What teachers actually deliver daily in classrooms — content, methods, and learning experiences in practice.

ClassroomTeacher-led

1.4 Learnt Curriculum

All changes that occur in learners from school experience — what students actually absorb through interaction with teachers, peers, and the institution.

OutcomesStudent-side

1.5 Hidden Curriculum

Lessons learned indirectly — not formally taught. Unwritten values and norms picked up in school. Example: punctuality, discipline, respect.

ImplicitInformal

1.6 Null Curriculum

Knowledge and skills not taught in school — intentionally or unintentionally. Example: Financial literacy, coding — if not taught, students miss it entirely.

AbsentGap

ཤེས་རིག་གི་ཡིད་ཆེས་བཞི། 4 Educational Philosophies

Why it matters: Without knowing the 4 educational philosophies, curriculum developers cannot identify national needs or create purposeful learning frameworks. These philosophies are the intellectual foundation of any curriculum.

The Four Educational Philosophies

① Essentialism — ཉེར་མཁོ་གཙོར་བཏོན།

Focus on essential skills needed in society: reading, writing, math, science, history, vocational skills. Teacher is the subject expert. Content decided by teachers, not student interest.

Teacher-centeredCore subjects

② Perennialism — རྒྱུན་གནས་གཙོར་བཏོན།

Focus on timeless, universal truths — knowledge that is true across all time and place (e.g. impermanence, rivers flow from mountains). Emphasis on classic works and religious/moral core.

Timeless truthUniversal

③ Progressivism — ཡར་འཕེལ་གཙོར་བཏོན།

Focus on student interest and ability. Student-centered learning. Teacher as facilitator (not authority). Standards based on individual growth, not comparison. Child-friendly school concept.

Student-centeredRousseau

④ Social Reconstructionism — མི་སྡེ་ལེགས་བཅོས།

Education must transform society. School as agent of social change. Address inequality, injustice, and social problems. Students as future changemakers.

Social changeReform

Essentialism vs Progressivism — Key Differences

AspectEssentialismProgressivism
FocusEssential skills for societyStudent interest & ability
ContentDecided by teacher/authorityBased on student needs
Teacher RoleSubject expert & modelFacilitator & guide
AssessmentStandardized comparisonIndividual growth
TheoristOlivia (1988)Rousseau

Perennialism — Key Characteristics

  • Teaches knowledge that remains true regardless of time and place
  • Example of perennial truth: rivers flow from mountains (ཆུ་མོ་གངས་ལས་འབབ།)
  • Example in ethics: impermanence (མི་རྟག་པ) — always relevant
  • Religious/moral core is permanent: cooperation and respect (ཁ་ཐུན་བཏང་ནི)
  • Does NOT focus on vocational/temporary skills
Exam Tip: For Dzongkha curriculum at senior level — Perennialism is linked to preserving cultural and Buddhist values. Progressivism is linked to child-friendly schools (ད་རེས་ནངས་པར་ཨ་ལུ་དང་མཐུན་པའི་སློབ་གྲྭ།).

རྩ་གཞུང་གི་གཞི་མི་སྡེ། Society as Curriculum Foundation

Core Idea: Society must be considered in curriculum design. Curriculum is a mirror of society's priorities — it asks: "What kind of citizens do we want for our future?"

Why Society Must Be Considered in Curriculum

  • Meet society's needs — prepare citizens for real community roles
  • Preserve culture and identity — ངོ་རྟགས་དང་ལམ་སྲོལ་ཉམས་སྲུང། (Bhutan: language, traditions)
  • Create a happy society — དགའ་སྐྱིད་ལྡན་པའི་མི་སྡེ།
  • Respond to social change — adapt to new trends (digital age, climate crisis)
  • Prepare responsible citizens — workers, leaders, and representatives
  • Solve social problems — present AND future challenges (drug abuse, junk food, etc.)
  • Society supports education — government, parents, community fund and run schools

The Continuous Cycle — Society ↔ Curriculum ↔ School

  1. 1Society identifies its needs and values
  2. 2Curriculum developers design learning to address those needs
  3. 3Schools implement the curriculum
  4. 4Students grow and contribute back to society
  5. 5Society changes → curriculum must be revised

How Curriculum Should Respond to Society

Prepares for life

Develops skills for real-world challenges — not just exam knowledge.

Preserves identity

Protects Bhutanese culture, language, and values across generations.

Adapts to change

Digital literacy, environmental sustainability, global citizenship.

Solves problems

Addresses inequality, environmental degradation, social disharmony.

Pedagogical Approaches for Society-Based Curriculum: Experiential Learning · Project Method · Problem-Based Learning · Inquiry-Based Learning · Collaborative Learning · Service Learning · Culturally Responsive Teaching · Integrated/Interdisciplinary Approach

སེམས་ཁམས་རིག་པ། Psychological Foundations

Key Idea: Learning = changes in internal cognition and external behaviour. All three dimensions of a child must be developed equally: cognitive, affective, psychomotor.

4.1 Behaviourism — ཕྱིའི་བྱ་སྤྱོད་གཞིར་བཞག

Classical Conditioning — Pavlov

Learning through stimulus-response associations. Repeated exposure creates conditioned responses. Example: Bell rings → dog salivates.

PavlovStimulus-Response

Operant Conditioning — Skinner

Learning through reinforcement and punishment. Positive reinforcement increases behaviour; punishment decreases it. Example: Stars/rewards in classroom.

SkinnerReinforcement

4.2 Cognitivism — ནང་སེམས་གཞིར་བཞག

Piaget — ཞོན་པེ་ཇེ།

4 stages of cognitive development: Sensorimotor → Preoperational → Concrete Operational → Formal Operational. Curriculum must match the child's developmental stage.

Stages

Kohlberg — ཀཱོལ་བཱག

Moral Development theory. 6 stages of moral reasoning from obedience to universal ethical principles. Relevant for values education in Dzongkha curriculum.

Moral

Vygotsky — ZPD

Zone of Proximal Development: the gap between what a student can do alone vs. with help. Curriculum should target this zone — challenge without overwhelm.

Scaffolding

Maslow's Hierarchy

Students must have basic needs met before they can learn. Curriculum must consider students' physical safety and belonging needs.

Needs

Gestalt Theory

Learning is holistic — understanding the whole pattern, not just parts. Relevant for integrated curriculum design.

Holistic

Rogers — Student-centered

Emphasizes freedom to learn, self-directed learning, and meaningful education. Aligns with Progressivism.

Self-directed

རྩ་གཞུང་རྩོམ་ཐངས་ཀྱི་དཔེ། Curriculum Development Models

6.1 Tyler's Model (1949) — ཏྲའི་ལར།

Approach: Top-down. Starts with clear objectives. Also called the "Objectives Model."
  1. 1Define Objectives: What should students learn? (from students, society, and subject specialists)
  2. 2Select Learning Experiences: Activities that help achieve objectives
  3. 3Organize Learning Experiences: Continuity, sequence, integration
  4. 4Evaluate: Did students meet the objectives?

6.1b Taba's Model (1962) — ཐིའུ་བཱ།

Approach: Bottom-up. Starts from classrooms (teachers and students). Inductive — grassroots model.
  1. 1Diagnose Learner Needs
  2. 2Formulate Objectives
  3. 3Select Content
  4. 4Organize Content
  5. 5Select Learning Experiences
  6. 6Organize Learning Experiences
  7. 7Evaluate

3 Levels of Educational Purpose

① Aims — སྤྱིར་བཏང་དམིགས་ཡུལ།

Broad, general purpose of education. Long-term, aspirational direction. Example: "Develop responsible citizens for Bhutan."

BroadLong-term

② Goals — རྩ་གཞུང་དམིགས་ཡུལ།

More focused than an aim. Clear, specific, and measurable at the programme level. Example: "Students will demonstrate reading fluency in Dzongkha by Grade 7."

SpecificProgramme level

③ Objectives — ལས་དོན།

Specific, measurable outcomes for lessons. Must include an action verb. Written using SMART or ABCD format.

MeasurableAction verb

Behavioural vs Non-Behavioural Objectives

TypeCharacteristicsExample
Behavioural ObjectiveHas clear action verb, observable, measurable (SMART/ABCD)"Students will identify 5 types of Dzongkha verbs in a sentence."
Non-Behavioural ObjectiveNo clear action verb, vague, hard to measure"Students will understand grammar."

ABCD Format for Writing Objectives

A — Audience

Who? The Grade 7 students...

B — Behaviour

What action verb? ...will identify...

C — Condition

Under what conditions? ...given a written text...

D — Degree

How well / standard? ...with 80% accuracy.

ནང་དོན་གདམ་ཁ་དང་བཅའ་སྒྲིག Content Selection & Organisation

Chapter 8: Principles for selecting content (Tyler & Schubert). Chapter 9: Organising that content — vertically (continuity/sequence) and horizontally (integration).

8.1 Tyler's Principles for Selecting Content

  • Validity — Content must be accurate and truly represent the subject
  • Significance — Must contribute to core ideas and concepts
  • Balance — Breadth and depth must both be served
  • Relevance — Must connect to students' real-life experiences
  • Learnability — Must be within students' ability and developmental range
  • Interest — Should engage and motivate learners

9.1 Two Types of Content Organisation

Vertical Organisation (གོང་འོག་)

Continuity: Same content/skill revisited repeatedly over time. Sequence: Content ordered logically — simple to complex, known to unknown.

Example: Dzongkha verb forms taught in Grade 5, revisited and deepened in Grade 7.

Spiral Curriculum

Horizontal Organisation (ཕན་ཚུན་)

Integration: Connecting content across subjects at the same level. Cross-curricular links for holistic understanding.

Example: Teaching water in Dzongkha, Science, AND Social Studies simultaneously.

Interdisciplinary

Curriculum Design for Learner Development

  • Content must match the learner's cognitive, emotional, and physical development stage (Piaget)
  • Curriculum should scaffold learning — build on prior knowledge
  • Content should challenge without overwhelming (ZPD)
  • Diverse cultural backgrounds and inclusive education must be considered
